The compliance gate

A proxy that decides whether an ad campaign is allowed to send traffic, before it sends any.

WherePublabs, InWeb team at InMobi
WhenAug 2025 – Feb 2026
StackPython, FastAPI, Redis, gunicorn, HMAC, LLM
My roleDesigned and built it; a senior engineer collaborated on the production refactor

The problem

The business bought traffic on one network and monetised it with search ad units on another. The monetising network has policies about what the landing page and the creative may say, and the first you hear about breaking them is a restriction on the account.

The failure is asynchronous, which is what makes it expensive. By the time a policy problem surfaces the campaign has been live for hours or days and the traffic is already spent. Reporting tells you what went wrong long after you could have stopped it.

So the check has to move from after the report to before the click. That means something in the request path that knows the approval state of every campaign. It also has to be fast enough that nobody notices it is there.

Constraints I had to build inside

  • It's in the click path. Every millisecond is a real user waiting, so approval state had to be a memory lookup rather than a database query.
  • Approval isn't permanent. An advertiser can swap a creative after approval, which quietly invalidates the decision that let it through.
  • Six networks, six shapes. Each ad platform exposes campaigns, ad accounts and creatives differently, and some of them lie about what's fetchable.
  • Multiple accounts, one Redis. Campaign state for different ad accounts could not be allowed to leak across the boundary.
  • Humans had to stay in it. A fully automatic gate that wrongly blocks a good campaign costs real money, so there had to be a review path.

How it works

The proxy owns the URL that the ad's creative points at. A click arrives with the landing page and its UTM parameters encoded into the proxy path, so the proxy resolves the campaign it belongs to before it decides anything.

Signed requests

Anything that can write approval state can decide what serves, so requests into the validation surface are HMAC-signed. The proxy recomputes the signature per campaign source and rejects anything that doesn't match. Secrets moved to environment variables early, and I stripped the hardcoded fallback defaults that had let the service start up in an unsafe state.

The LLM auto-approval layer

The expensive cases are the ones in the middle: copy that is not clearly fine and not clearly a breach, which a person has to read.

So I put a model in front of the queue. It reads the creative and the policy context and either clears the campaign or leaves it for a human, so the review queue only holds the cases where a judgment call is needed. The model's job is to clear, never to reject on its own.

You can poke at a simplified version of that decision path on the home page. It runs in the browser with an illustrative rule set, not the production one.

The watchdog

Approval describes a creative at a moment. Change the image and the approval is describing something that no longer exists. A polling service walks the networks, compares creatives against what was approved, and pauses anything that has drifted.

This was the part that generated the most production pain. Naive comparison flagged campaigns constantly, because the networks re-encode thumbnails and hand back different URLs for the same asset. Getting the false-positive rate down meant normalising creatives before comparing them and adding a thumbnail fallback for the cases where the network wouldn't serve the original.

Decisions, and what each one cost

Redis as the source of truth on the hot path

Approval state is small, hot and read on every click, so it lives in memory rather than behind a database query.

cost Redis becomes a hard dependency of serving. I later hit exactly that problem on a different service, which is why the platform now has a circuit breaker around it.

Replacing KEYS with SMEMBERS, and prefixing every key

The first version enumerated campaigns with KEYS, which walks the entire keyspace and gets slower as the business grows. Campaign sets moved behind explicit set membership, and every key got a prefix scoped to its ad account.

cost Set membership has to be maintained on write, so deletes and moves became more code. Worth it: the account prefix is also what makes tenant isolation enforceable rather than assumed.

A poller on cron instead of a long-running loop

The poller was converted to one-shot execution so cron owns the schedule. A crashed run is then just a missed tick that the next one recovers from.

cost Detection latency is bounded by the cron interval rather than being continuous. For creative drift, minutes are acceptable.

A per-network adapter rather than one generalised client

Meta, Google, TikTok, Bing, Outbrain and Taboola disagree about almost everything. I built per-network configuration and diagnostics instead of forcing a single abstraction.

cost Six things to maintain. It paid for itself the first time Meta stopped listing an ad account under /me/adaccounts and that network needed a direct-access fallback nothing else wanted.

What it does now

  • Meta was integrated and production-tested first, then Google Ads, TikTok Ads, Bing, Outbrain and Taboola.
  • Campaigns are validated before their traffic reaches the search units, so a policy breach is a blocked click rather than an account problem.
  • The review workflow gives the business team an approve or reject with a comment, and the model clears the volume that doesn't need them.
  • It left behind a reusable compliance framework that other traffic sources can plug into.
What I'd do differently

This one ran long. Requirements kept moving while I was still ramping up on the domain, and a senior engineer worked with me on the refactor that made it production-ready. Two things changed in how I work because of it: I get a design review and written agreement on requirements before I write code, and I roll out incrementally instead of integrating six networks and then testing.

The technical lesson was narrower. I under-validated the third-party APIs before building against them. Outbrain in particular had creative-format constraints that I found during integration rather than before it, and that ordering is what cost the time.
